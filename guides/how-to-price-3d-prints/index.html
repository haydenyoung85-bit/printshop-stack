How to price 3D prints

A cost-plus method for pricing 3D prints: material, machine time, labor, failures and marketplace fees, with a clearly labelled worked example.

Most pricing mistakes in small print shops come from counting only filament. A price that holds up has to cover every cost of making and selling one unit, plus the margin you want. This guide walks through a cost-plus method you can put in a spreadsheet.

The basic formula

Work out a unit cost, add your margin, then make sure the price still clears once the sales channel takes its cut:

unit cost = material + machine time + labor + packaging + failure allowance
price     = unit cost + margin + channel fees + shipping you don't charge for

Channel fees are usually a percentage of the sale price, so they grow as the price grows. Calculate them on the final price, not on the cost.

1. Material

Divide what you paid for a spool by its net filament weight to get a cost per gram, then multiply by the grams the job uses. Your slicer reports estimated filament use for each plate after slicing. Include supports, brims, and the purge waste from multi-color prints, because you paid for that filament too.

Spool prices vary a lot by brand, material and sales channel. That is why our filament listings say "see site" instead of quoting a number. Use your own receipts.

2. Machine time

Machine time covers electricity plus wear on the printer. For electricity: average power draw (watts) × print hours ÷ 1000 = kWh, then multiply by your rate per kWh. Your rate is on your utility bill. The U.S. Energy Information Administration publishes average rates by state if you need a reference point (source 4). Average draw depends on the printer, bed temperature and material, so measure it with a plug-in power meter if you can.

For wear, add a small hourly amount to cover nozzles, build plates, belts and eventually replacing the printer. A simple approach: estimate what you spend on parts and replacement over a period, divide by the print hours in that period, and charge that rate per hour.

3. Labor

Count the hands-on minutes: preparing files, starting and clearing the printer, removing supports, sanding or painting, quality checks and packing. Multiply by an hourly rate you would be happy to be paid. Labor is often the biggest cost on small, fiddly items, and the easiest one to forget.

4. Failure allowance

Some prints fail. Track your own failure rate for a few weeks, then spread the cost of failed attempts across the good units: divide the cost of one attempt by your success rate. Failure-detection tools can cut down the waste. See the print-farm software guide.

5. Channel fees

On Etsy, sellers pay a $0.20 listing fee per listing. Listings last four months, and each extra quantity sold is charged again (source 1). When an item sells there is a 6.5% transaction fee on the item price plus any shipping and gift wrapping you charge (source 1). Etsy Payments processing is separate and depends on where your bank account is. For a US bank account it is 3% + $0.25 (source 2, source 3). Sales that come from Offsite Ads carry an extra advertising fee (source 3). Check these pages yourself before you set prices, because fees change.

On your own store you pay a platform subscription and payment processing instead of a marketplace commission. Our Shopify listing has the plan prices we found (source 5).

Worked example (illustrative numbers only)

Example. Every input below is hypothetical, picked to make the arithmetic easy. They are not market prices or measurements.

Material: $20 spool ÷ 1,000 g × 50 g used$1.00
Electricity: 150 W × 3 h = 0.45 kWh × $0.15/kWh$0.07
Wear: 3 h × $0.10/h$0.30
Labor: 10 min at $24/h$4.00
Packaging$0.60
Subtotal$5.97
Failure allowance at a 90% success rate ($5.97 ÷ 0.9)$6.63

Suppose you list this item on Etsy from the US at $15.00 with $5.00 shipping charged, and the order is not from Offsite Ads. Using the fee rates cited above: transaction fee 6.5% × $20.00 = $1.30. Processing 3% × $20.00 + $0.25 = $0.85. Listing fee $0.20. Fees total $2.35. If the actual postage costs you $5.00, what's left is $20.00 − $5.00 − $2.35 − $6.63 = $6.02 before income tax. Processing is charged on sales tax as well (source 2), so real figures will differ slightly.

Sanity checks before you publish a price

  • Look at comparable listings to see what buyers already pay. Use them as a check, not as your cost basis.
  • Recalculate whenever filament prices, your electricity rate or the marketplace fees change.
  • Price multi-color and large prints separately. Purge waste and long print times change the math.
  • Make sure discounts and free shipping still leave a margin after fees.
